## Membership Inference Attack Methods: 
Anwar, Alba, Richard and Jim compared shadow-model, quantile-regression and canary-based attacks, concluding that Lyra remains a useful reference method but its suitability depends on computational resources, model access and the number of available shadow models.

LiRA Applicability: Alba said LiRA can be computationally intensive and may be impractical in some settings. Richard added that performance depends on the number of shadow models and whether access is white-box or black-box; 
- RMIA may be preferable with only one or two shadow models, while QMIA may be relevant when shadow models cannot be built.

Canary-Based Methods: Jim explained that newer approaches insert known synthetic records or canaries into training data when only one training run is possible, then test whether the model treats those records differently from equivalent variants.  
Alba noted that intentional insertion could also be viewed as a poisoning-related risk, particularly for deployed large language models.
	
Quantile Regression Limitations: Richard explained that quantile-regression attacks avoid the need for shadow models but still require a model mapping original features to confidence scores. Their performance can decline as the tested target model becomes more complex, potentially requiring a similarly large attack model.

Implementation Status: Jim said the team has implementations for quantile regression and other attacks, while RMIA is not yet implemented and remains on the to-do list. They agreed that comparing available scripts and attacks will help identify further infrastructure requirements.

## Generative AI Assurance Roadmap: 
Jim and Alba agreed that generative AI and large language models require a dedicated roadmap because the risk landscape is changing rapidly, with next year’s work expected to focus on productionising assurance methods and understanding parameter-efficient fine-tuning risks.
	
Risk Taxonomy: Alba explained that the current taxonomy has focused mainly on traditional models because their risks and assurance methods are better understood. Generative AI introduces additional risks and uncertainties, so the taxonomy must be extended without attempting to address every issue simultaneously.
	
Roadmap Requirement: Jim reiterated that the field is changing too quickly to promise a complete set of general-AI assurance tools within the project timeframe. A key deliverable is therefore a roadmap, with more mature work already available for restrictive Category A models and conventional classifiers and regressors in Category C.
	
Fine-Tuning Risks: Jim is investigating low-rank adaptation and other parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods with the Transpect team. A key question is whether successive users could add further adapters to an already fine-tuned model and thereby reproduce or expose aspects of earlier training data.

Federated ML Productionisation: Jim said next year’s project work will emphasise productionising federated machine-learning assurance rather than only conducting research. The Melody project’s practice of defining training and test sets before using Flower AI appears promising because it creates a basis for running attacks consistently.

External Assurance Work: Jim reported involvement in the HDR Alliance AI Assurance Special Interest Group and the International Population Data Linkage Network. They are also reviewing Imperial’s TRE case study involving model access controls, continuous accuracy checks and model recall criteria, and coordinating an SDC Reboot response to ICO guidance on AI, data protection, anonymisation and TREs.
